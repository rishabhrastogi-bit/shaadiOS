Parental alienation: meaning, signs, and what really helps children

When parents separate, a child can end up caught between them. Parental alienation is the name often used when one parent’s words or actions turn a child against the other. Here is what it means, why the label needs care, and what actually helps children.

What is parental alienation?

Parental alienation describes a child rejecting one parent, without a good reason, because of the other parent’s influence: what they say about that parent, or how they block or spoil time together. It can happen with either parent, mothers or fathers, and sometimes grandparents or other relatives play a part.

It is not a medical diagnosis. “Parental alienation syndrome” is a phrase you’ll see often, but the World Health Organization does not list parental alienation as a health condition; it describes it as a term used mainly in legal disputes. In India, the Supreme Court discussed “parental alienation syndrome” in a 2017 custody case, and in 2024 it clarified that parental alienation is not a syndrome that can be diagnosed, but a process of manipulation, and that courts should look at specific alienating behaviour.

A child’s resistance can have many reasons

When a child doesn’t want to see a parent, alienation is only one possible reason. Others include:

  • Loyalty conflict: the child feels that enjoying time with one parent hurts the other.
  • Normal phases: children, especially teenagers, may prefer one home for friends, school or routines.
  • Upset about the separation, or anger at the parent they blame for it.
  • How a parent behaves: harshness, unreliability, missed visits or a new partner the child struggles with.
  • Real fear or harm. This must always be taken seriously.

Experts and a 2023 United Nations report have warned that the “alienation” label is sometimes used to dismiss a child’s or parent’s genuine concerns about violence. Understanding why a child feels as they do comes before any label.

Behaviours that hurt children (from either parent)

These behaviours put a child in the middle. Any parent can slip into them under stress; noticing them in yourself is a strength.

  • Criticising or mocking the other parent in front of the child.
  • Asking the child to choose sides, or to carry messages, or to report back on the other home.
  • Making visits or calls difficult without a genuine reason, or making the child feel guilty for enjoying them.
  • Sharing adult details of the conflict, money or court case with the child.
  • Telling the child, directly or not, that the other parent doesn’t love them or isn’t safe, when that isn’t true.
  • Rewarding the child for rejecting the other parent.

Worried your child is being turned against you? What helps, and what to record

  1. Stay steady and loving. Keep showing up on time, keep calls warm and short, and don’t pressure your child to explain their feelings.
  2. Don’t fight back through your child. Criticising the other parent in return only deepens the loyalty conflict.
  3. Keep calm, factual records. Note dates, times and what happened: visits cancelled, calls not answered, messages sent. Facts, not opinions. Records like these help a counsellor, mediator or court see a pattern of specific behaviour, which is what Indian courts look for.
  4. Get professional help early. A children’s counsellor or family therapist can help your child, and family counselling or mediation can help both parents agree a plan.
  5. Legal help. If contact keeps being blocked, a family lawyer can advise you. For free legal aid, call 15100.

This is general information, not legal advice. Every family and case is different; courts decide custody and contact on the welfare of the child.

If your child is afraid of the other parent

Listen calmly and take it seriously. Let your child speak in their own words, and avoid leading questions or repeated questioning, which can confuse a child. Speak to a children’s counsellor, and if a child is in immediate danger, call 112.

Questions people ask

What is parental alienation?

Parental alienation describes a child rejecting one parent without a good reason because of the other parent’s influence, such as criticising that parent or blocking time together. It can happen with either parent.

Is parental alienation syndrome a real diagnosis?

No. The World Health Organization does not list parental alienation as a health condition and describes it as a term used mainly in legal disputes. In 2024 the Supreme Court of India said parental alienation is not a syndrome that can be diagnosed, but a process of manipulation, and that courts should look at specific alienating behaviour.

Can parental alienation be against the mother or the father?

Yes. Either parent, mother or father, can be on either side of alienating behaviour. What matters is the behaviour and its effect on the child, not the parent’s gender.

How do you prove parental alienation in India?

Courts look at specific behaviour and the child’s welfare, not labels. Keep calm, factual records with dates (cancelled visits, blocked calls, messages), involve a children’s counsellor or family therapist, and get legal advice; free legal aid is available on 15100. This is general information, not legal advice.

What should I do if my child refuses to visit the other parent?

Listen calmly to understand why, without pressure or leading questions. Resistance can have many reasons, including loyalty conflict, normal phases or real fear. A children’s counsellor can help, and if a child is in danger, call 112.

What are signs of parental alienation?

Signs can include a child suddenly rejecting a parent they used to love without a clear reason, repeating adult words or complaints, showing no guilt about hurtful behaviour, and refusing contact with that parent’s whole family. A professional should look at the full picture, because resistance has many possible causes.
